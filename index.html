The alkali metals

Group 1: reactions with water and the trend

Drop a piece of each metal into water and compare. Switch to the atom view to see why reactivity increases down the group. Rubidium and caesium are predictions from the trend.

Key equations

metal + water → metal hydroxide + hydrogen2Na + 2H2O → 2NaOH + H2

Breaking it down

  1. All Group 1 atoms have one electron in the outer shell. They lose it to form a 1+ ion, which is why they react in the same way.
  2. With water they make hydrogen gas and a metal hydroxide that dissolves to give an alkaline solution. Universal indicator turns purple. Hydrogen gives a squeaky pop with a lit splint.
  3. Down the group the reaction is faster: lithium fizzes, sodium melts, potassium burns with a lilac flame.
  4. Why more reactive down the group: more electron shells, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and is more shielded by the inner shells. The attraction is weaker, so the electron is lost more easily.
  5. Melting points fall down the group (Li 181 °C, Na 98 °C, K 63 °C). Rubidium and caesium follow the trend: lower melting points and more violent reactions.
  6. They are soft, have low density, and are stored under oil.

Common traps

  • Saying potassium is more reactive "because it has more electrons". The reason is the distance and shielding of the outer electron.
  • Forgetting state symbols: Na(s), H2O(l), NaOH(aq), H2(g).
  • Not balancing: 2Na + 2H2O → 2NaOH + H2. Check the hydrogens.
  • Writing "it explodes" for lithium. Match the observation to the metal.

Try this: switch to the atom view and step from Li to Cs. What happens to the pull on the outer electron? Then tick the indicator and compare how fast the water turns purple.

The halogens

Group 7: displacement reactions and the trend

Key equations

Cl2 + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br2Cl2 + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br2

Breaking it down

  1. Halogens exist as diatomic molecules (Cl2, Br2, I2). Chlorine is a pale green gas, bromine a red-brown liquid, iodine a grey-black solid. Boiling points rise down the group.
  2. A more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide. Reactivity order: chlorine > bromine > iodine.
  3. Solution colours: bromine water is orange, iodine solution is brown, chlorine water is pale green or colourless.
  4. In the ionic equation the potassium ions are spectator ions. The halogen molecule gains electrons (reduction) and the halide ion loses them (oxidation).
  5. Why reactivity falls down the group: a halogen atom has to gain an electron. Lower in the group there are more shells, so the incoming electron goes into a shell further from the nucleus with more shielding and is attracted less strongly.
  6. Predict: fluorine is more reactive than chlorine. Astatine is a black solid and less reactive than iodine.

Common traps

  • Mixing up the trends: Group 1 gets more reactive down the group, Group 7 gets less reactive.
  • Writing atoms (Cl) instead of molecules (Cl2) in equations.
  • Writing "iodine is displaced" as an observation. The observation is that the solution turns brown.
  • Saying "no reaction" without a reason. Give it: bromine is less reactive than chlorine.

Try this: test all nine combinations. Which halogen displaces both of the others? Which displaces neither?

Chromatography and Rf values

Run the solvent up the paper, then identify the dyes in an unknown ink by comparing spots with reference dyes. Change the solvent and watch the Rf values change. Switch on the mistakes to see what goes wrong.

Key equation

Rf = distance moved by the substance ÷ distance moved by the solvent front

Breaking it down

  1. Draw the baseline in pencil and put a small spot of each sample on it. Stand the paper in solvent with the solvent level below the baseline.
  2. The solvent is the mobile phase and moves up the paper, the stationary phase. Substances that dissolve better in the solvent and stick less to the paper travel further.
  3. Take the paper out before the solvent reaches the top and mark the solvent front straight away.
  4. A pure substance gives one spot. A mixture gives several. Spots at the same height with the same colour as a reference are the same substance.
  5. Rf is a ratio, so it has no units and is between 0 and 1. Measure the spot from the baseline to the centre of the spot.
  6. Rf depends on the solvent. Compare spots only from the same run, or use the same solvent.

Common traps

  • Measuring from the bottom of the paper instead of from the baseline.
  • Writing units on Rf, or giving a value above 1.
  • Saying the solvent has to be above the baseline. If it is, the dyes wash into the solvent.
  • Using pen for the baseline. Ink is also a mixture of dyes, and it runs up the paper.
  • Forgetting that colourless spots need a locating agent such as ninhydrin or UV light.

Try this: run Ink 2 in water and identify its dyes. Then switch to ethanol. Why can't you compare a water run with an ethanol run?

Gases in the atmosphere

Composition of air and measuring oxygen

Heat copper while air is passed back and forth between two gas syringes. The copper takes the oxygen out. Change the heating time and see how the answer depends on doing the experiment properly.

two gas syringes and heated copper

Key equations

2Cu + O2 → 2CuO% oxygen = (volume used ÷ starting volume) × 100

Breaking it down

  1. Dry air is about 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, 0.9% argon and 0.04% carbon dioxide by volume.
  2. Hot copper reacts with oxygen to make black copper(II) oxide. Nitrogen and argon do not react, so the volume of gas falls by about one fifth.
  3. Pass the air back and forth over the copper so that all the oxygen reacts, and use excess copper.
  4. Let the apparatus cool to room temperature before reading. Hot gas takes up more space, so a hot reading is too big and the oxygen looks too low.
  5. Other methods use damp iron wool (rusting) or phosphorus burning in a closed container. Each removes oxygen so the volume falls.
  6. Carbon dioxide is only a tiny fraction of air but is a greenhouse gas. Rising levels may contribute to climate change.

Common traps

  • Giving "20% oxygen, 80% nitrogen" as the exact answer. Know 78% and 21% and the small gases.
  • Dividing by the final volume instead of the starting volume.
  • Forgetting to cool the apparatus, or stopping heating too early.
  • Saying the copper "absorbs" the air. It reacts with the oxygen only.

Try this: set the heating time to 1 minute and read the oxygen. Then untick the cooling box at 6 minutes. What happens to the result, and why?

Combustion of elements

Sulfur and burning elements in oxygen

Burn magnesium, hydrogen or sulfur in a gas jar of oxygen. Add water with universal indicator to see whether the oxide is acidic or basic. Then explore how sulfur dioxide in the air makes rain more acidic.

gas jar of oxygen
Burn

Key equations

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO2H2 + O2 → 2H2OS + O2 → SO2

Breaking it down

  1. Magnesium burns with a bright white flame and leaves a white powder, magnesium oxide. Metal oxides are basic.
  2. Hydrogen burns with a pale blue flame and makes water. A squeaky pop shows hydrogen. Water turns white anhydrous copper(II) sulfate blue.
  3. Sulfur burns with a blue flame to make sulfur dioxide, a colourless, choking gas. Non-metal oxides are acidic.
  4. Sulfur dioxide dissolves in water to give an acidic solution, so universal indicator turns red or orange. Magnesium oxide solution is alkaline (blue). Water is neutral (green).
  5. Acid rain: fuels such as coal and oil contain sulfur. Burning them releases sulfur dioxide, which dissolves in rain. Acid rain damages limestone buildings, kills fish and harms trees.
  6. Reduce it by removing sulfur from fuels, or by passing waste gases through calcium oxide or calcium carbonate (flue gas desulfurisation).

Common traps

  • Saying sulfur burns with a "yellow flame". It is blue in oxygen.
  • Confusing sulfur dioxide with carbon dioxide as the cause of acid rain. Carbon dioxide causes climate change.
  • Unbalanced equations. 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO needs the 2s.
  • Calling every oxide acidic. Metal oxides are basic, non-metal oxides are acidic.

Try this: burn each element, tick the indicator box and compare the three colours. Then slide the sulfur dioxide level and watch the rain pH.
